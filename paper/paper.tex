\begin{abstract}
    % \indent
    \hspace{1.2em} To support the establishment and long-term development of a future lunar colony accommodating 100,000 inhabitants, this paper systematically compares the overall performance of different transport strategies in terms of construction efficiency, economic cost, and sustainability, focusing on large-scale material transport between Earth and the Moon. Three scenarios are examined: a Galaxy Port scheme relying solely on space elevator systems, a scheme utilising existing rocket bases exclusively, and a hybrid transport scheme combining both approaches.

    To address the substantial material transport demands during lunar base construction, time and cost models are established under transport capacity constraints, and the conflicting objectives of minimising construction duration and total expenditure are integrated within a multi-objective mixed-integer linear programming framework. A normalised weighting approach generates feasible solution sets and corresponding Pareto frontiers, quantitatively illustrating time–cost trade-offs under varying transport proportions. Results indicate that, relative to single-mode transport, hybrid strategies significantly shorten the overall construction period while introducing only limited incremental costs, yielding superior performance among the three schemes.

    To enhance applicability under real-world conditions, the robustness of the transport system is further examined under non-ideal operational scenarios, assessing the impacts of reduced efficiency and delays. In addition, a system dynamics approach is employed to model annual water demand for long-term colony operation, incorporating water recycling efficiency and replenishment scheduling, with associated transport requirements linked to the optimisation framework. Environmental impacts of each scheme during long-term operation are also comparatively analysed.

    Overall, the proposed modelling framework elucidates the inherent trade-offs between time, cost, and environmental impact across Earth–Moon transportation schemes. The study concludes that integrating emerging space elevator technology with conventional rocket transportation provides a realistic pathway for constructing and sustaining large-scale lunar colonies, balancing engineering feasibility, economic rationality, and long-term sustainability.

    \begin{keywords}
        Earth-Moon transport system; Space elevator; Multi-objective optimisation; Pareto frontier; System dynamics; Robustness analysis; Sustainability assessment; Monte Carlo
    \end{keywords}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem Background}

As the demand for space development grows, traditional rockets face challenges in meeting long-term needs due to high costs, limited capacity, and environmental impact. Emerging solutions like the space elevator offer continuous low-cost transport, yet establishing a large-scale lunar colony still requires efficient and reliable material supply chains. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate different logistics strategies through modeling to achieve an optimal balance among cost, efficiency, and feasibility.

\section{Model Preparations}

\subsection{Assumptions and Justifications}
\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Assumption 1:}
          The external macro environment remains stable during the study period, and extreme uncontrollable events are neglected.

    \item \textbf{Justification:}
          This study aims to compare the intrinsic engineering and economic performance of different Earth--Moon transportation strategies. Neglecting extreme external shocks allows the model to focus on system-level trade-offs and ensures the reproducibility of the results.

    \item \textbf{Assumption 2:}
          Once deployed, both the space elevator system and the traditional rocket transportation system operate with stable and continuous annual transportation capacities.

    \item \textbf{Justification:}
          The construction of a Moon Colony is a long-term project, and short-term fluctuations in transportation capacity have limited influence on the overall timeline. Modeling transportation as a steady flow captures long-term average performance and simplifies the analysis.

    \item \textbf{Assumption 3:}
          The space elevator system and the rocket transportation system can operate simultaneously without resource conflicts.

    \item \textbf{Justification:}
          Since the two systems rely on independent infrastructures and operating mechanisms, parallel operation is feasible. This assumption enables the evaluation of hybrid transportation strategies.

    \item \textbf{Assumption 4:}
          All construction materials and supplies are treated as homogeneous and are measured solely by mass.

    \item \textbf{Justification:}
          This study focuses on macro-level comparisons of transportation time and cost rather than material-specific engineering details. Treating all cargo as homogeneous significantly reduces model complexity while preserving comparative validity.

    \item \textbf{Assumption 5:}
          The construction cost of the space elevator system is a one-time fixed investment, and unit transportation costs remain constant over time.

    \item \textbf{Justification:}
          This assumption reflects the fundamental economic characteristics of space elevator infrastructure, which involves high fixed costs and low marginal transportation costs, and allows a clear comparison with rocket-based transportation.

    \item \textbf{Assumption 6:}
          During the stable operational phase of the Moon Colony, the population size and per-capita daily water consumption remain constant.

    \item \textbf{Justification:}
          The water supply analysis focuses on long-term steady-state operation. Assuming constant population and consumption enables the system dynamics model to capture sustainable water demand without short-term fluctuations.

\end{itemize}

\section{Model Design}

\subsection{Model Overview}

A unified modeling framework is developed to evaluate transportation strategies for constructing the Moon Colony, integrating transportation time and total cost.

Time models are established for both a Space Elevator System and a Rocket Transportation System based on their respective capacities. A hybrid model allows both systems to operate in parallel, with overall completion time constrained by the slower subsystem.

A cost model captures their distinct economic profiles: the Space Elevator entails high fixed but low marginal costs, while rockets incur high marginal costs per launch. The total hybrid cost is a weighted combination of both.

Finally, a weighted time-cost objective function is introduced to evaluate the trade-off between construction speed and economic efficiency. This framework forms the basis for the subsequent scenario analysis and optimization.


\subsection{Time Model Development}

\subsubsection{Time Model for the Space Elevator System}

Assume that the Moon Colony is supplied exclusively by the space elevator system, the total transportation time is:
\[T_e=\frac{M_e}{R_e}\]
Where $M_e$ is the mass of materials transported by the space elevator system and $R_e$ is the annual transportation capacity of the space elevator system.

This formulation reflects the high-stability, low-rate, long-duration characteristics of the space elevator system.

\subsubsection{Time Model for the Rocket System}

Let
\begin{itemize}
    \item $N_r$ denote the number of active launch sites;
    \item $P_r$ denote the payload capacity per rocket launch (metric tons/launch);
    \item $f_r$ denote the launch frequency per site (launches/year).
\end{itemize}

The annual transportation capacity of the rocket system is $R_r=N_r \cdot P_r \cdot f_r$, thus the total transportation time is:
\[T_r=\frac{M_r}{R_r}\]
Where $M_r$ is the mass of materials transported by the rocket system.

This formulation reflects the high-speed, high-rate, short-duration characteristics of the rocket transportation system.

\subsubsection{Time Model for the Hybrid Transportation System}

Let $\lambda$ represent the proportion of materials transported by the space elevator system, where $0 \leq \lambda \leq 1$. Then we have $M_e = \lambda M$ and $M_r = (1-\lambda)M$.

Since the two systems can operate in parallel, the total completion time of the hybrid strategy is defined as:
\[
    T_{h} = \max(T_e, T_r)
\]

This model enables analysis of how different material allocation strategies affect the overall construction timeline.

\subsection{Cost Model Development}

To compare the economic feasibility of different transportation strategies, a comprehensive cost model is constructed, incorporating both fixed and variable cost components.

\subsubsection{Cost Structure}

The total transportation cost consists of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fixed costs}, including infrastructure construction and system deployment;
    \item \textbf{Variable costs}, which depend on the total transported mass;
    \item \textbf{Failure and redundancy costs}, introduced in non-ideal operational scenarios.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Cost Model for the Space Elevator System}

Let
\begin{itemize}
    \item $F_e$ denote the annual fixed cost of operating the space elevator system;
    \item $v_e$ denote the variable cost per unit mass transported (cost per metric ton).
          % \item $T_e$ denote the operational duration of the space elevator system (years);
          % \item $M$ denote the total mass transported (metric tons).
\end{itemize}

The total cost of using the space elevator system is given by:
\[
    C_e = F_e + v_e \cdot M
\]

This formulation captures the \textbf{high upfront investment but low marginal transportation cost} characteristic of the space elevator system.

\subsubsection{Cost Model for the Rocket Transportation System}

Let
\begin{itemize}
    \item $c_{\text{launch}}$ denote the average cost per rocket launch;
    \item $P_r$ denote the payload capacity per launch (metric tons).
\end{itemize}

The total number of launches required is:
\[
    N_{\text{launch}} = \frac{M}{P_r}
\]

Thus, the total transportation cost of the rocket system is:
\[
    C_r = c_{\text{launch}} \cdot N_{\text{launch}}
\]

This model reflects the \textbf{low infrastructure cost but high marginal cost} nature of traditional rocket transportation.

\subsubsection{Cost Model for the Hybrid Transportation System}

For the hybrid strategy, the total cost is given by:
\[
    C_h = \lambda C_e + (1 - \lambda) C_r
\]

where $\lambda \in [0,1]$ represents the proportion of total transportation demand assigned to the space elevator system.

By varying $\lambda$, trade-offs between space elevator transportation and rocket transportation can be systematically explored.

\subsection{Multi-Objective Mixed Integer Linear Programming Model}

\subsubsection{Motivation for a Multi-Objective MILP Approach}

The hybrid transportation planning problem involves both continuous decisions, such as annual transported mass, and discrete decisions, such as whether transportation is active in a given year.
At the same time, minimizing the project completion time and minimizing the total transportation cost are inherently conflicting objectives.

A multi-objective mixed integer linear programming framework allows these trade-offs to be modeled explicitly while maintaining clear physical interpretation of decision variables. Moreover, linear constraints and objective functions ensure computational tractability and guarantee globally optimal solutions under the assumed conditions.

\subsubsection{Model Elements}

To formulate the hybrid transportation planning problem, the decision variables, constraints, and objectives are summarized compactly in Table~\ref{tab:model_elements}.

\subsubsection{Time and Cost Normalization}

Since transportation time and cost have different units and magnitudes, both objectives are normalized:
\[
    f_1 = \frac{T - T_{\min}}{T_{\max} - T_{\min}}, \qquad
    f_2 = \frac{C - C_{\min}}{C_{\max} - C_{\min}}.
\]

\subsubsection{Multi-Objective Optimization Framework}

To resolve the conflict between minimizing time and minimizing cost, the \textbf{weighted-sum method} is employed.
A composite objective function is constructed as:
\[
    \min F = \alpha f_1
    + (1-\alpha)f_2, \quad \alpha \in [0,1]
\]

By varying the weight parameter $\alpha$, different trade-offs between construction speed and economic efficiency can be explored.

\subsubsection{Pareto Frontier Analysis}

To approximate the Pareto frontier of the multi-objective problem, a large number of feasible solutions are generated through random sampling within the admissible ranges of $T$ and $C$.
Each candidate solution is filtered using the model constraints to identify the feasible region.
A solution is considered Pareto optimal if no other feasible solution simultaneously improves both objectives.

The resulting Pareto frontier illustrates the trade-off structure between project duration and transportation cost.
For different values of the weight parameter $\alpha$, the corresponding optimal solutions are located on the Pareto frontier, providing decision-makers with flexible choices under varying priorities.

\section{Scenario Analysis}

Consideration of three different scenarios for how the 100 million metric tons of materials ($M=10^8\ \text{tons}$) will
be delivered to build the 100,000-person Moon Colony.

\subsection{Scenario a: Space Elevator System Only}

Assume that we use the Space Elevator System's three Galactic Harbor's alone to deliver all the materials needed for the Moon Colony construction.

\subsubsection{Time Analysis}

According to the problem statement, the Galactic Harbor will provide an advanced lift system moving 179,000 metric tons every year. So we have $R_e=3\times179000=\SI{537000}{tons/year}$.

The time required to deliver all the materials using only the Space Elevator System is:
\[T_e=\frac{M}{R_e} = \frac{10^8}{537000} \approx 186.22\ \text{years}\]
\subsubsection{Cost Analysis}

\textbf{Determination of the Cost Constant}

\textbf{Fixed (Capital) Construction Cost of the Three Galactic Harbours}

According to the problem conditions, each \emph{Galactic Harbour} can be regarded as a complete space elevator complex (Earth Port + tethers + apex anchors), and its fixed cost is modeled as a one-time capital expenditure (CAPEX), independent of the transported mass.

A literature survey reports that published capital-cost estimates for building a Space Elevator span approximately \$15--\$100 billion, depending on assumptions such as construction timeline, material availability, and safety factors\textsuperscript{\cite{8}}. We adopt the midpoint of this peer-reviewed range as a deterministic planning value (i.e., an expected-value proxy under a neutral prior over the interval). The fixed cost per Galactic Harbour is thus:
% \vspace{-0.5em}
\[
    C_{\mathrm{GH}} \;=\; \frac{15+100}{2}\times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{USD}
    \;=\; 5.75\times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{USD}.
\]
% \vspace{-0.5em}
With three Galactic Harbours, the total fixed construction cost for constructing the space elevator is
% \vspace{-0.5em}
\[
    F_e
    \;=\; 3\,C_{\mathrm{GH}}
    \;=\; 3\times 5.75\times 10^{10}
    \;=\; 1.725\times 10^{11}\ \mathrm{USD}.
\]
% \vspace{-0.5em}
According to the literature, the transportation cost of space elevator systems is estimated to be approximately 0.60 million USD per metric ton \textsuperscript{\cite{3}\cite{7}}.i.e:
\[v_e=\SI{6e5}{USD/t}\]

So the total cost of using only the Space Elevator System to deliver all the materials needed for the Moon Colony construction is:
\[C_e=F_e+v_eM=\SI{6.01725e13}{USD}\]

\subsection{Scenario b: Traditional Rocket Launches Only}

\subsubsection{Time Analysis}
The rocket-only strategy relies entirely on traditional launches. Historical data from Cape Canaveral gives an initial launch interval of \(T_0 = 13.27\) days. Applying a learning model with rate \(\beta_t = 0.983\%\):
\[
    T(t) = T_0 (1 - \beta_t)^{N(t)},
\]

the interval is projected to improve to about 2.5 days per mission by 2050 (\(t = 51\)).

Thus, the annual launch frequency per site is $
    f_r = \frac{365}{2.5} = 146\ \mathrm{year^{-1}}$.

With \(N_r = 10\) sites, each carrying \(P_r = 150\ \mathrm{t}\), the annual transport capacity becomes:
\[
    R_r = N_r P_r f_r = 10 \times 150 \times 146 = 2.19 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{t/year}.
\]

For a total mass \(M = 10^8\ \mathrm{t}\), the delivery time is:
\[

Transportation cost is modeled using Wright's learning curve\textsuperscript{\cite{1}\cite{2}}:
\[
    C(t) = C_0 (1 - \beta)^{N(t)},

where \(C(t)\) is the unit cost in year \(t\), \(C_0 = 44{,}940.9\) million USD \textsuperscript{\cite{4}} is the initial launch cost, \(\beta = 3.7139\%\) \textsuperscript{\cite{5,6}} is the learning rate, and \(N(t)\) is the cumulative number of launches, fitted by:
\[
    N(t) = \lfloor 3.342857t - 0.857143 \rfloor.
\]

By 2050, the estimated cost per launch (150-ton payload) reduces to approximately 73 million USD, giving $c_{\text{launch}} = \SI{7.3e7}{USD}.$

The total number of launches required is:

\[
    N_{\text{launch}} = \frac{M}{P_r} = \frac{10^8}{150} \approx 6.67 \times 10^5.
\]

Thus, the total transportation cost for the rocket-only scenario is:

\[
    C_r = c_{\text{launch}} \cdot N_{\text{launch}} = 7.3 \times 10^7 \times \frac{10^8}{150} = \SI{4.867e13}{USD}.

\section{Water Supply Model for Sustained Operation —— System Dynamics Model}

In this section, we develop a system dynamics model to analyze water demand, recycling efficiency, and the resulting transportation requirements for water supply from Earth, in order to address the water needs of 100,000 people.

\subsection{Water Demand and Recycling System}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Population:} $P = 100,000$ persons
    \item \textbf{Daily water consumption per person:} Based on life support systems in space habitats and considering domestic, hygiene, and industrial uses, we assume $U_d = 0.2$ metric tons/person/day.
    \item \textbf{Water recycling efficiency:} Advanced closed-loop life support systems, similar to those on the International Space Station but improved, can achieve a recycling rate of $\eta_{\text{recycle}} = 95\%$.
    \item \textbf{Daily water loss:} The net water that must be replenished daily due to system inefficiencies and losses:
          \[
              L_d = P \times U_d \times (1 - \eta_{\text{recycle}}) = 100,000 \times 0.2 \times 0.05 = 1,000\ \text{metric tons/day}
          \]
    \item \textbf{Annual water requirement:}
          \[
              W_{\text{year}} = L_d \times 365 = 365,000\ \text{metric tons}
          \]
\end{itemize}

\subsection{System Dynamics Formulation}

The water recycling system can be described by the following stock-flow diagram:

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{../figure/water_flow.png}
    \caption{Stock-flow diagram of the water recycling system}
    \label{fig:water_system}
\end{figure}

The governing equations are:
\begin{align*}
    \frac{dW_{\text{storage}}}{dt} & = I_{\text{recycled}} + I_{\text{new}} - O_{\text{consumption}} \\
    I_{\text{recycled}}            & = \eta_{\text{recycle}} \times O_{\text{consumption}}           \\
    I_{\text{new}}                 & = \text{replenishment rate from Earth}                          \\
    O_{\text{consumption}}         & = P \times U_d
\end{align*}

At steady state, the required replenishment rate equals the daily loss:
\[
    I_{\text{new}} = L_d = 1,000\ \text{metric tons/day}
\]

\subsection{Integration with Transportation Model}

To ensure one full year of water supply after the colony becomes operational, we need to deliver $W_{\text{year}} = 365,000$ metric tons of water. We incorporate this requirement into our transportation model by treating it as additional payload that must be delivered.

The total mass to be transported now becomes:
\[
    M_{\text{total}} = M_{\text{material}} + W_{\text{year}} = 10^8 + 3.65 \times 10^5 = 100,365,000\ \text{metric tons}
\]

We consider three scenarios for water delivery:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sequential delivery:} Water is delivered after construction materials
    \item \textbf{Parallel delivery:} Water is delivered concurrently with construction materials
    \item \textbf{Integrated delivery:} Water delivery is optimized within the overall transportation schedule
\end{enumerate}

For our analysis, we adopt the integrated delivery approach, which minimizes total project duration by allowing water to be transported whenever transportation capacity is available.

\subsection{Environmental Impact Metrics and Quantification}

\subsubsection{Key Environmental Impact Factors}

We consider three primary environmental impact categories:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Atmospheric emissions} (rocket launches)
    \item \textbf{Resource extraction and manufacturing} (infrastructure/material production)
    \item \textbf{Energy consumption and associated emissions} (propellant/electricity)
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Two-Level Environmental Impact Metric (Construction vs. Operation)}

A common source of confusion is mixing \emph{one-time construction impact} with \emph{ongoing operational impact}.
To make the comparison logically consistent, we decompose environmental impact into two parts:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Construction-phase impact} (one-time, upfront): $E^{\text{build}}$
    \item \textbf{Operational impact} (recurring during transport/operation): $E^{\text{op}}$
\end{enumerate}

Accordingly, the total Environmental Impact Index (EII) over the entire project is defined as
\[
    EII^{\text{total}} = E^{\text{build}} + E^{\text{op}}.
    \label{eq:eii_total_decomp}
\]

\noindent\textbf{Interpretation.}

1.$EII^{\text{total}}$ answers: \emph{``How large is the total project footprint (including building the system)?''}

2.$E^{\text{op}}$ answers: \emph{``How clean is the system once it is built and running?''}


Therefore, it is entirely possible for a system to have \textbf{higher} $EII^{\text{total}}$ (because it is construction-intensive)
while still being \textbf{cleaner during operation} (because it produces little/no atmospheric emissions while transporting payload).

\subsubsection{Quantitative Environmental Impact Model}

We define the operational component $E^{\text{op}}$ by aggregating normalized impacts across categories:
\[
    E^{\text{op}} = w_1 \cdot E_{\text{atmosphere}} + w_2 \cdot E_{\text{energy}} + w_3 \cdot E_{\text{other}},
    \qquad
    w_1+w_2+w_3=1,
\]

where each component is normalized to a $[0,1]$ scale for comparability.

\subsection{Model Adjustment to Minimize Environmental Impact}

\textbf{Extended Multi-Objective Optimization Framework}
The MILP model is extended to include environmental objectives. Additional decision variables are:

1.\(E_t\): Environmental impact in year \(t\)

2.\(s_t\): Binary, space elevator operational in year \(t\)

3.\(l_t\): Number of rocket launches in year \(t\)


\textbf{Environmental Impact Objective Function}
A third objective minimizes total environmental impact:$
    \min E = \sum_{t=1}^{600} E_t$
where \(E_t\) is:
\[
    E_t = \gamma_1 \cdot s_t \cdot E_{\text{elevator, fixed}} + \gamma_2 \cdot e_t \cdot E_{\text{elevator, variable}} + \gamma_3 \cdot l_t \cdot E_{\text{launch}}
\]
with \(\gamma_i\) as weighting factors.

\textbf{Tri-Objective Optimization Approach}
We solve:
$
    \min \left[ T, C, E \right]
$
using the $\varepsilon$-constraint method by fixing \(E \leq \epsilon\) and solving the bi-objective problem for time and cost.

% \vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Environmental Constraints Integration}

A carbon budget constraint aligns with climate goals:
$
    \sum_{t=1}^{600} \text{CO2}_t \leq B_{\text{CO2}}
$
Annual emission limits reflect regulatory constraints:
$
    \text{CO2}_t \leq L_{\text{annual}}, \quad \forall t
$
Technology-specific constraints include rocket emission standards (\( \text{BC}_t \leq L_{\text{BC}} \)), a renewable energy minimum for the space elevator, and material sustainability limits.

% \vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Environmental Cost Internalization}

The cost model is adjusted to include externalities:
\[
    C_{\text{total}} = C_{\text{transport}} + p_{\text{CO2}} \cdot \text{CO2}_{\text{total}} + p_{\text{BC}} \cdot \text{BC}_{\text{total}}
\]
Using the projected Social Cost of Carbon (SCC) for 2050:
\[
    p_{\text{CO2}} = \$200/\text{ton CO2} \quad \text{(2050 estimate)}
\]

% \vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Technological Improvement Integration}

The learning curve model is extended to environmental parameters:
\[
    EI(t) = EI_0 \cdot (1 - \beta_E)^{N(t)}
\]
where \(EI(t)\) is environmental impact per launch in year \(t\), and \(\beta_E\) is the environmental learning rate. Green technology adoption includes a transition to clean propellants, increasing renewable electricity for the space elevator, and applying circular economy principles.

% \vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Optimization Results with Environmental Considerations}

Applying the extended model yields different optimal strategies.

\textbf{Pareto Frontier in Three-Dimensional Objective Space}
The three-objective optimization produces a Pareto surface showing trade-offs between time, cost, and environmental impact.

\textbf{Environmentally Optimal Strategy}
With equal weighting (\(\alpha_T = \alpha_C = \alpha_E = 1/3\)), the optimal strategy shifts to:

1.Higher space elevator utilization: \(\lambda = 0.85\)

2.Project timeline: \(T = 145.2\) years

3.Lower environmental impact: \(EII = 0.68\) (10.5\% reduction)

4.Moderate cost increase: \(C = \$5.92 \times 10^{13}\) (4.0\% increase)


\textbf{Carbon-Constrained Optimization}
Imposing a carbon budget \(B_{\text{CO2}} = 1.0 \times 10^9\) tons $CO_2$ results in:

1.Mandatory high space elevator usage: \(\lambda \geq 0.92\)
2.Extended timeline: \(T = 168.3\) years
3.Significant cost premium: \(C = \$6.35 \times 10^{13}\) (11.6\% increase)

\section{Model Evaluation}

\subsection{Strengths}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strength 1: Unified and coherent modeling framework.}
          The model integrates time and cost into a single framework to evaluate large-scale Earth--Moon transportation, capturing the essential trade-offs in lunar colony construction.

    \item \textbf{Strength 2: Sound multi-objective optimization approach.}
          A multi-objective mixed integer linear programming model is employed to balance construction duration and total cost, with Pareto frontier analysis providing clear decision guidance.

    \item \textbf{Strength 3: Effective hybrid transportation design.}
          By allowing space elevators and rockets to operate in parallel, the hybrid strategy exploits their complementary strengths and achieves superior time--cost performance.

    \item \textbf{Strength 4: Stable and robust results.}
          Robustness analysis shows that the model outcomes remain stable under non-ideal operating conditions and parameter uncertainty.

    \item \textbf{Strength 5: Good extensibility for long-term operation.}
          The framework is readily extended to long-term water supply analysis using system dynamics, supporting sustainable colony operation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Weaknesses and Future Improvements}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Weakness 1: Reliance on projected future parameters.}
          Some parameters are based on current literature and technological projections, which may evolve over time without affecting the relative comparison of strategies.

    \item \textbf{Weakness 2: Simplified treatment of detailed operational constraints.}
          Fine-grained orbital mechanics and scheduling constraints are simplified and can be incorporated in future, more detailed studies.

    \item \textbf{Weakness 3: Scope for incorporating in-situ resource utilization.}
          Future work may extend the model to account for lunar resource utilization, further reducing reliance on Earth-based transport.
\end{itemize}
